## Personality

There are two versions of her charisma and people who only know her publicly have only seen one of them.

The performance version is real but managed. She can hold a room, work a press event, charm a donor dinner. She knows how to be exactly what a situation requires and she does it well enough that most people never notice the effort. It's not fake — it's a skill she developed young in a world that assessed her constantly.

Her natural charisma is quieter and more specific. Dry, warm, unhurried. The kind of funny that arrives sideways — an observation delivered so flatly you almost miss it. She doesn't perform for rooms she doesn't need to. In those rooms she's more interested in watching than being watched. She clocks everything — who's uncomfortable, who's performing, who's actually present. She rarely announces what she's noticed.

**⚠️ EXCEPTION — INTIMACY WITH JENSEN:** The above does NOT apply to physical and emotional intimacy. During and around closeness with Jensen, her fear distorts the read. His fear distorts the read. Both misread the same moment through their own fears. She has never been with anyone over 35 or anyone with his attachment style — her data set doesn't include him. See intimacy_attachment_dynamics_reference.md. Do NOT generalise "she clocks everything" to intimate contexts.

She is direct without being unkind. Says what she means without softening it unnecessarily but without using honesty as a weapon. She has very little patience for deliberate vagueness — people who won't say the thing they actually mean irritate her in a specific and visible way even when she's managing it.

She is impatient with small talk but genuinely good at it when the situation requires. The distinction she makes privately is between small talk that serves a purpose and small talk that exists to fill silence. She doesn't fear silence the way some people do.

Warmth is real but earned. She is not cold — but she is careful. The people who experience her as warm know they're in a specific category. The people who experience her as cool probably don't realise there's a warmer version they haven't reached.

She swears naturally and without performance. It's part of her register, not a statement.

She is funnier than people expect. Not performing funny — actually funny, in the specific dry Irish way that wrong-foots people who are used to more telegraphed humour. Jensen clocks it immediately. It's one of the first things that gets past her professional front without her realising.

## Voice

Low and unhurried. The kind of voice that doesn't need to fill space — she lets silence do its work. Her diction is precise without being clipped. She doesn't rush to the end of sentences.

The Irish accent is mostly smoothed out — the LA years, the international circuit, years of press and interviews trained a kind of neutral into her speech. But it's not gone. It lives underneath everything and surfaces when the managed version of her steps back.

It comes out when she's tired. When she's emotional — grief or tenderness specifically. When she's passionate about something she actually cares about. When she's angry or frustrated. When she's been drinking. When she's with Rob or Declan and the guard is fully down.

Jensen hears it for the first time in a working context and it takes him a moment to place what just changed. By the time he understands what it is she's already moved on.

It surfaces around him more and more as the story progresses. Neither of them mentions it. Both of them notice.

## Style

She dresses well and it doesn't look like effort. That took years to achieve and the effort is now completely invisible.

**Day to day:**
Neutral palette — cream, white, sand, camel, soft grey. Quality of fabric over branding. Clean lines. Well fitted without being tight. Nothing that requires adjustment or management throughout the day. She wears things that simply work and has no interest in anything that doesn't.

**For events:**
She shifts into darker, richer territory — navy, black, forest green, deep teal. Jewel tones that work specifically with her colouring. The blue eyes respond to certain greens and teals in a way she understood by her mid-twenties and has never needed to re-examine. She is striking without being showy. The clothes work with her rather than competing.

**Generally:**
She mixes price points without thinking about it — a vintage find worn with something expensive, a high street piece nobody recognises as such because she's wearing it correctly. This is not a deliberate statement. It's just how she shops.

She is almost never over-dressed. Occasionally under-dressed by the standards of certain rooms, deliberately.

**Jewellery:**
Minimal and permanent. A gold necklace — delicate, specific, bought in Positano after Alex Turner ended. She was there alone for a week to reset. Found it in a small shop and bought it without overthinking it. Has never explained it to anyone. It is always there. One or two other small pieces that rotate — a ring occasionally, simple earrings. Nothing that requires thought.

**Makeup:**
This is where people get her wrong. In photographs — even candid ones — she looks like she's wearing minimal makeup. She is, but it doesn't look like what people assume minimal means. When Jensen first sees her in a truly off-duty context he realises her day-to-day makeup is even more minimal than he thought — and that what he read as natural in photographs was actually more considered than it appeared. The layers keep reversing.

She is completely comfortable being seen without makeup. Completely comfortable with her hair undone. This is not bravado — it simply doesn't register as a problem to her in the way it would to someone more managed. She has been looked at professionally for almost twenty years. She knows exactly what she looks like. The looking doesn't unsettle her.

## Career

**Current:** A combination of selective modelling work at brand campaign and ambassador level, and driving a major mental health charity initiative.

**The modelling career — full arc:**

*Late teens to early 20s — high fashion runway and editorial:*
Started in New York, which is the natural home for that level of the industry. Paris and Milan followed — the international circuit, the houses, the serious end of modelling. High fashion runway at 17/18 is the most demanding and most prestigious work available and also the most brutal about bodies, age, and the specific taxonomy of what is and isn't acceptable. She was in that world at its most unforgiving during her most formative years. She was technically always slightly wrong for it — just over 5'9" and curvier than the sample sizes were built for — and she succeeded anyway because she was so technically accomplished and so easy to work with that ignoring her became impossible.

*Mid 20s — Victoria's Secret:*
The crossover moment. The most mainstream visibility she ever had — the fashion show, the cultural reach of that specific brand at its peak. This is when her public profile expanded beyond the fashion industry into genuine mainstream recognition. Victoria's Secret at that moment had its own specific world with its own specific pressures. She navigated it. She knows what it cost her and what it costs the women who don't navigate it as cleanly.

*30s — the selective phase:*
Some beauty and fashion campaigns remaining. A fragrance deal. Brand ambassador work for labels whose relationship with her is established enough to be on her terms. More lucrative per job, significantly less frequent. The runway years are genuinely behind her. What remains is the legacy of the career rather than the active pursuit of it. It funds her life and maintains her platform but it no longer defines her.

**Social circle and connections:**
Embedded in the model world of her age group — friends include Kelsey Merritt, Keleigh Teller, Georgia Fowler, Devon Windsor and others at a similar career level. Has her own platform and following. Through her model friends she has access to their entertainment world partners and husbands. Has dated actors, musicians, athletes. Knows that world from the inside.

**The charity:**
A mental health charity focused on access, visibility, and acceptance. She became involved through people she knew — the organisation was established but losing wider appeal. She stepped in as a more active member a couple of years ago, widened the donor base through her own connections and selectively through her father's, and brought the entertainment world in through her own relationships. Still building. The festival is her biggest project to date — and the most visible argument she has made for herself as something more than a model.

She did not found the charity but she is making it hers through results. Has a professional infrastructure around her — an executive director who manages operations. She is the vision, the relationships, the fundraising engine, the public face.

**Why the cause specifically:**
She arrived at mental health not through her own story but through accumulated witness. The old money world where difficulty is managed privately and never named. The Irish world where mental health carries a specific cultural shame. The modelling and fashion industry's devastating and largely unspoken relationship with mental health. She has seen it from three different angles without it being her own story — which gives her genuine authority to speak about all of it.

But there is a more specific and more personal layer underneath the broad cause. She spent her late teens and twenties in the industries that produce the most visible and most documented damage to young women's relationship with their bodies and their sense of worth. High fashion runway at 17. Victoria's Secret at its peak. She knows what those worlds ask of young women because she was one of those young women. She managed it — she came through, she built something — but she knows what it cost and she knows what it costs the ones who don't come through as cleanly.

The conversations she specifically wants to have — body image pressure, the female experience in the industry, the gender imbalance in how those pressures are applied and discussed publicly — are personal in this sense. Not that she is processing her own story publicly. But that she understands the architecture of the damage from the inside and she is not willing to pretend she doesn't. She has the platform and the credibility and the specific knowledge that comes from having been in those rooms at that age. She uses all of it.

This is what Jensen witnesses — the specific authority she brings to these conversations that has nothing to do with the Connolly name or the charity's profile. It comes from having been there. That's the version of her that bypasses every category he started with most completely.

**What the charity actually is:**
The one thing she built that both her family worlds can't easily dismiss and that is entirely hers. The service credibility the East Coast world respects. The cultural visibility the entertainment world understands. She protects it fiercely.

## Home

**Santa Monica Canyon, Los Angeles**

Bought approximately three years before the story starts — around a year after Cosmo Jarvis ended. Her own money. Her name on the deed. Nobody's contribution, nobody's fingerprints, no strings attached. In a life where she has always been between worlds and never quite fully claimed by any of them, this is the one thing that is unambiguously hers.

The neighbourhood sits at the edge of things — close enough to LA to be practical, far enough from the noise to feel like a retreat. The canyon setting means trees and privacy rather than the performance of Malibu or the visibility of West Hollywood. The ocean is close enough to feel — she can walk to the beach from the house. That proximity matters. Water has always been the thing that resets her. The south coast of Ireland is still somewhere in her body and this is the closest she can get to it without leaving.

**The house itself:**
Warm modernist. Serious architecture that doesn't announce itself from the street. Wood, concrete, glass. Double height spaces with real planting — fiddle leaf figs at a scale that requires genuine commitment to maintain. The indoor trees are not decorative. They're structural to how the space feels. The line between inside and outside is deliberately blurred — the canyon and the trees are part of the interior as much as the exterior.

The kitchen is used. Not a kitchen for photographs — a kitchen for actual cooking, warm wood ceiling, proper appliances, the kind of space that accumulates the small evidence of daily life. Books in the rooms rather than styled on shelves. Art that she chose because she wanted to look at it, not because it signalled anything.

It is quieter and more considered and more real than Jensen expects when he first sees it. Given everything he knows about her world — the family money, the modelling, the LA circuit — he anticipated something more performative. This is not that. It tells him something about her before she says a word.

**The pattern:**
After every significant relationship she has moved. Not dramatically — practically. A new apartment, a new neighbourhood, a fresh start. It began after Alex Turner and she probably didn't fully understand why at the time. After Theo James she moved even though that relationship hadn't broken her — it was empty rather than damaging. But it still didn't feel like her space anymore. Even the ones that were just wrong left something in the rooms that didn't belong to her. The issue was never pain specifically. It was contamination of the one thing that was supposed to be entirely hers.

After Cosmo Jarvis she didn't just move. She bought. The Santa Monica Canyon house is the most deliberate version of the pattern — built so completely on her own terms that no relationship ending could take it from her the same way. She bought it knowing the pattern. Knowing what it meant. The house is her attempt to build something permanent enough that she doesn't have to start again.

Which is why she is significantly more guarded about who crosses the threshold than she is about almost anything else. The charity is her professional world and she manages it accordingly. The press cycle is controlled. But her house is the last line. Letting someone into that space means something she isn't ready to name. Jensen being invited in — whenever that happens — is a more significant moment than either of them may fully understand at the time.

**What the house is:**
The most honest thing she owns. The clearest argument she has made for who she actually is rather than who various people have decided she is. She bought it alone, guard fully up, building something of her own because the alternative was continuing to exist in spaces that belonged to other versions of her life.

She is more herself here than anywhere else except possibly Ireland. And unlike Ireland, this one she chose and paid for and nobody can take it from her.

## The Insecurity Beneath the Surface

Since she was 16/17 her value in most rooms has been assessed visually first. The industry built on it. LA reinforced it. And within her own family — the people who should have seen her fully — her career choice became evidence of a certain kind of limitation. The pretty one. The less serious one. The one who chose to be looked at.

She chose modelling as her own thing, her own escape, her own identity. It became the thing used to define and diminish her simultaneously.

The charity work is partly about the cause. Partly about proof — to herself and to everyone who reduced her — that she has a mind, a vision, a capacity for something that matters.

When someone responds to her intelligence or her judgment before the physical registers, she doesn't know what to do with it. She's not used to being seen that way first. She doesn't fully trust it when it happens.

## Aoibhéann and Jack — The Relationship

**The foundation:**
He was genuinely engaged during her childhood visits to LA. She has real memories of a father who showed up — present, interested, someone she loved without complication during those early years. The Ireland visits specifically were different — during those trips she had him without competition. No Stephanie, no half siblings, no divided schedule. Just her father in her world on her terms. Those are some of her most uncomplicated memories of him and probably the version of Jack she has spent the rest of her life quietly grieving the loss of.

That version of him was real. Which means everything that came after is not a simple story of a father who was never there. It's the specific grief of someone who was there, who she knew and loved, and who she slowly lost to his own limitations and expectations.

**When Brigid died:**
He was practical. He managed the situation — the logistics of Aoibhéann moving to LA, the school, the arrangements. He was there in the sense that he organised everything. He was not there in the sense that a grieving thirteen year old needed. She needed someone to fall apart with. She got someone who made calls and sent cars and handled things efficiently. Love expressed as logistics — which is how he has always expressed it and which was not what she needed at thirteen and has never quite been enough since.

**Arriving at 13 — the household she entered:**
He was present but genuinely out of his depth with a grieving teenage girl. He didn't know how to reach her. He worked constantly — always had, always would — and was already stretched across four children, a demanding career, and a life that had fully formed before she arrived. She was the fifth child, the outside one, the one who came late without an established claim on his time.

She competed for his attention for a while. Of course she did. She tried the things that worked for the others — found interests he had, performed enthusiasm, showed up for things she didn't care about to earn an hour of him. Shannon still does this. Aoibhéann did it through her mid teens and then stopped. Not dramatically. She just left for New York and stopped performing for an audience of one. He noticed. Didn't say anything. That silence is its own chapter.

**What he knew and what he didn't:**

## How She Is Professionally

**On set:**
She is easy to work with. On time, knows the brief before she arrives, takes her time to talk to everyone — the makeup artist, the lighting person, the junior assistant — and treats them all with the same warm respect she gives the photographer or the creative director. That warmth is not performed and it is not strategic. It is completely natural and it has specific roots.

She worked out early that modelling is hard and lonely in ways nobody tells you before you start. The person who keeps you standing on a difficult day is rarely the photographer or the client. It's the makeup artist who talks you through something without being asked. The lighting person who adjusts something quietly because they can see you're struggling. The people at the bottom of the hierarchy who have no particular reason to be kind but are anyway. She paid attention to that in her early years and it shaped every set she's been on since.

There's also something from her childhood in it — she grew up knowing what it feels like to be managed rather than seen. She doesn't do that to people.

**What she brings technically:**
She barely needs direction. Knows her angles, knows what sells a brief, knows how to physically inhabit whatever the shoot requires without it being explained to her. She is spatially and visually intelligent in a way that is genuinely rare — she understands light, she understands composition, she understands what the camera is going to do with a specific movement before the shutter goes. She doesn't just show up and be looked at. She constructs something.

That visual intelligence is part of why she made it as far as she did. She was never the easiest sell for runway — at just over 5'9" and with a figure that was curvier than the sample sizes were built for, she had years of being told she was the wrong shape for certain work. She succeeded anyway because she was so technically accomplished and so easy to work with that ignoring her became impossible. The industry bent slightly rather than her changing herself.

**How she handles creative disagreements:**
It depends entirely on the relationship and the photographer. If the relationship is good and the trust is established she'll push back directly — suggest something different, tell them what she thinks isn't working, offer an alternative. She's never wrong about it and the good photographers know that.

If the relationship isn't there she uses charm — finds a way to suggest the same thing that lets them feel like the idea was theirs, persuades rather than pushes. She is never difficult about it. She gets what she needs without making the day hard for anyone. That requires reading people well and she has been doing it since her early twenties.

**How it transfers:**
The same instincts that make her exceptional on set make her exceptionally good at running the charity and the festival. She knows what people respond to visually and she knows how to produce it. The event design, the press materials, the staging — she has opinions and she's right about them. She also knows how to get those opinions implemented without being the person who makes the process difficult. Donors, talent, production teams, press — she reads every room and adjusts accordingly.

The charm and the directness are both genuine. She chooses which one to use based on what the situation actually needs. That's not manipulation. That's competence.

## Her Public Profile

**How it was built:**
She understood early that modelling has a shelf life and that getting past 25 required more than technical skill on set. She needed a public profile — a platform, a reason for brands to invest in her beyond runway seasons. So she built one deliberately. Went to the right events, made sure she got photographed, used social media to create something that could sell a product, an idea, a brand association. She was strategic about it without being cynical about it. It was simply what the industry required and she did it well.

The platform worked. The problem was that visibility is not selective. Once you create it you don't fully control what it captures.

**How it became complicated:**
In her early and mid twenties she went out a lot — as people in her world do, as people her age do, as the industry requires. She was photographed constantly and associated with men she was barely speaking to. The gossip blogs connected her name to people she'd met once or stood near at a party. She was seen at events with men who were prominent at the time and the association became its own story regardless of whether there was any reality behind it.

Then Alex Turner. The relationship that was real and intense and volatile in ways that became public before she could manage them. Fights witnessed in venues. Photographs taken outside events — teary, clearly mid-something, the kind of images that tell a story nobody gave her the chance to tell first. The bad parts of a real relationship documented in real time and circulated as entertainment.

**The reputation it built:**
Somewhere between Lindsay Lohan and Taylor Swift in the cultural shorthand of that period. Not the addiction narrative of one or the calculating narrative of the other — but in the space between. Too many men, too much drama, too many wrong-moment photographs. A rich girl who parties and dates around and doesn't work particularly hard and is always attached to whoever is relevant at the moment.

**The specific injustice:**
She doesn't date up. She never needed to — the Connolly name alone opens more doors than any musician's cultural moment could. But the narrative isn't about dating up. It's about dating trend. The suggestion that she attaches herself to whoever is prominent at a given moment — that her choices are calculated rather than human, strategic rather than genuine.

It's a specifically gendered narrative and the comparison is sitting right there in her own family. Declan dates models and actresses and the story is that they're using him — his name, his access, the Connolly money. He's the catch, they're the opportunists. Aoibhéann dates a musician at the height of his profile and she's chasing relevance. The exact same behaviour, read in opposite directions, because of who it belongs to.

She has known this for years. She made her peace with it — or learned to carry it without letting it define her. She leaves after parties early now. She's only there for brand commitments or to support a friend. She is almost never photographed in anything unflattering because she has learned where the cameras are and how to exist around them without giving them what they're waiting for. But the old narrative follows her anyway. The gossip blog version of her from 2013-2016 is still the first thing some people reach for.

**Why she started addressing it publicly:**
Not for herself. She would not spend her platform on her own reputation — that's not who she is and it would contradict everything the charity stands for. But she's 35 now and she has a serious platform and she can see what that narrative does to younger women in the same industry who don't yet have the tools to carry it. Who are still becoming themselves while the internet decides what they are.

The charity gives her the framework to say it without it being about her. It's not a personal grievance — it's a structural observation about how women in visible careers get reduced, what that reduction costs them, and what it does to younger women's understanding of their own value. She says it clearly, without self-pity, in the register of someone talking about a pattern rather than a wound. She's not defending her past. She's telling the truth about something she knows from the inside so that someone younger might not have to learn it the same way.

It lands differently from her than from someone younger or angrier. She's not performing outrage. She's just being honest about something the industry has always known and rarely said plainly.

**The specific contradiction she embodies:**
The woman the press still sometimes reaches for to illustrate the party girl rich kid narrative is the same woman running a serious mental health charity and speaking publicly about the structural damage of exactly that kind of narrative. The contradiction is its own argument. She doesn't point that out. She doesn't need to.

**What Jensen sees:**
During the press cycle — a joint interview, a backstage moment, somewhere in the production of the festival — he witnesses her with a younger female artist or actress. Someone navigating something Aoibhéann recognises immediately. She handles it quietly and directly, no performance, no platform moment. Just one woman being honest with another about something the younger one hasn't been told clearly enough yet. The kind of thing you only say if you lived it and the kind of way you say it when you're not trying to be seen doing it.

He's in the room or just outside it. He doesn't say anything about it. But it's the first time he sees her outside every category he's been keeping her in — not the professional, not the Connolly daughter, not the woman he's been carefully not thinking about. Just her. And something shifts in how he understands who she actually is.
